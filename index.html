1.1

Scarcity

MKT-1.A.1MKT-1.A.2

Exam skill: 1.A, describe economic concepts, principles, or models. Expect to define scarcity and connect it to the need to make choices.

Scarcity means there are not enough resources to meet everyone’s wants and needs. It is not about being poor. A billionaire, a government, and a student all face it, because wants keep going and resources run out. Scarcity is why every decision has a trade-off.

Factors of production
The resources used to make goods and services. The AP framework names land (natural resources), labor (human work), and capital (human-made tools, equipment, and buildings). Many textbooks add entrepreneurship as a fourth factor.
Trade-off
Giving up one thing to get another. Because resources have more than one use, using them for one purpose means not using them for another. Topic 1.5 turns this into opportunity cost.
Rival good
One person’s use leaves less for everyone else, like a slice of pizza or an hour of a teacher’s time.
Non-rival good
One person’s use does not reduce what others can have, like established knowledge. This is why some factors of production, such as established knowledge, may not be scarce.

Two common traps

  • Scarce does not mean rare. Tap water is not rare, but using it for one purpose means not using it for another, so it is scarce.
  • Money is not capital. In AP Economics, capital is physical: machines, tools, buildings. Money is what you use to buy capital.

Scarcity is a binding constraint, and constraints have prices

In AP Economics, scarcity is the fact that forces trade-offs. In advanced economics it becomes a constraint in an optimization problem. A consumer picks the bundle (x, y) that maximizes utility U(x, y) = α ln x + (1 − α) ln y subject to the budget pxx + pyy = B. The Lagrange multiplier λ on that constraint is its shadow price: how much the best achievable utility rises if scarcity eases by one more dollar.

Show the derivation
Set up the Lagrangian: L = α ln x + (1 − α) ln y + λ(B − pxx − pyy) First-order conditions: α/x = λpx     (1 − α)/y = λpy So pxx = α/λ and pyy = (1 − α)/λ. Plug into the budget: 1/λ = B, so λ = 1/B,   x* = αB/px,   y* = (1 − α)B/py The maximum utility is V*(B) = ln B + constant, so dV*/dB = 1/B = λ. That equality is the envelope theorem: the shadow price is the slope of the value function. Because 1/B shrinks as B grows, an extra dollar is worth less when you have more. That is diminishing marginal utility, which you meet in topic 1.6.

Go further: the multiplier as the marginal utility of income, and the envelope theorem, are covered in MIT OpenCourseWare 14.03 (Autor), lecture 4, and in graduate texts such as Mas-Colell, Whinston, and Green’s Microeconomic Theory.

Non-rival ideas and increasing returns

The AP framework notes that established knowledge may not be scarce because it is non-rival. Advanced growth theory builds on that fact. Creating an idea takes scarce resources once, a fixed cost F. After that, serving one more user costs almost nothing, so the average cost F/n keeps falling as users n grow. That is a source of economies of scale, and it connects forward to natural monopoly in topic 4.2. A related but distinct idea, increasing returns to scale, shows up when you scale all the inputs of a production function.

A simple version of the idea behind Romer (1990), in the spirit of Jones’s textbook treatments, puts ideas A into a production function: Y = A · Kα · L1−α. Scale only the rival inputs K and L and output scales one for one. Because A is non-rival, one stock of ideas serves every plant at once, so scaling all three inputs multiplies output by more than the scale factor. Romer’s own result goes further: because a non-rival input creates a non-convexity, price-taking competition cannot support the equilibrium, and he used monopolistic competition instead. You will see that market structure in topic 4.4.

Go further: Romer, “Endogenous Technological Change,” Journal of Political Economy 98(5), 1990, pp. S71–S102; Jones, “Growth and Ideas,” NBER Working Paper 10767, for the replication argument.

Prices as shadow prices: the planner’s problem

Imagine a benevolent planner splitting an amount X of one resource between two people to maximize welfare W = ω·a1 ln x1 + (1 − ω)·a2 ln x2, subject to x1 + x2 = X. Here ai is how much person i values the resource and ω is the weight the planner puts on person 1. At the best split, the weighted marginal utilities are equal, and that common value λ is the shadow price of the resource. A market price plays the same role: each buyer keeps buying until their marginal benefit falls to the price.

Show the derivation
Lagrangian, with X = 100: L = ωa1 ln x1 + (1 − ω)a2 ln x2 + λ(X − x1 − x2) First-order conditions give one common value: ωa1/x1 = (1 − ω)a2/x2 = λ Solving with x1 + x2 = X: x1* = Xωa1 / (ωa1 + (1 − ω)a2),   λ = (ωa1 + (1 − ω)a2) / X By the envelope theorem, λ = dW*/dX, the welfare value of one more unit of the scarce resource. In the market version, each person with utility ai ln xi plus money buys until ai/xi = p. Clearing the market gives p = (a1 + a2)/X and x1 = Xa1/(a1 + a2), which is the planner’s split when ω = 0.5.

Notice what the weight ω does. Moving it away from 0.5 changes who gets more, but not whether the resource is used up. Choosing ω is a value judgment about whose wants count more, which connects to the “who consumes” question. Market prices allocate according to willingness and ability to pay given existing endowments; they do not by themselves choose society’s ethical weights. Taxes and transfers can change the distribution.

Hayek’s knowledge problem
In “The Use of Knowledge in Society” (1945), Hayek argued the hard part of planning is not solving the math but gathering information that is scattered across millions of people. Prices compress that information. The planner’s information slider in the ticket simulation stands in for this problem.
Socialist calculation debate
Mises (1920) argued rational economic calculation is impossible without market prices for capital goods. Lange (1936–37) proposed a market-socialist reply using trial-and-error pricing. The debate is a classic on how systems coordinate.
Welfare theorems
First theorem: if consumers’ preferences are locally nonsatiated, every competitive equilibrium is Pareto efficient. The competitive-market conditions include, for example, no unpriced externalities, price-taking, and markets for every relevant good. Second theorem: with convex preferences and technologies (plus a technical condition), any Pareto-efficient allocation can be supported as a competitive equilibrium after suitable lump-sum transfers of wealth. In theory, equity can be set by ethics and coordination left to prices. In practice, truly lump-sum transfers are hard to find. In their modern, rigorous form the theorems were proved by Arrow (1951) and Debreu (1951), building on earlier work in welfare economics.
Mechanism design
Hurwicz, Maskin, and Myerson shared the 2007 Nobel Prize for this field, which designs rules, like auctions and allocation systems, so self-interested people reveal what a planner needs to know. Price, lottery, and planner in the ticket simulation are three simple mechanisms.

Go further: Hayek, “The Use of Knowledge in Society,” American Economic Review 35(4), 1945; Mises (1920) and Lange (1936–37) on socialist calculation; Arrow, “An Extension of the Basic Theorems of Classical Welfare Economics,” Proceedings of the Second Berkeley Symposium on Mathematical Statistics and Probability (University of California Press, 1951), pp. 507–532; Debreu, “The Coefficient of Resource Utilization,” Econometrica 19(3), 1951, pp. 273–292; Mas-Colell, Whinston, and Green, Microeconomic Theory (1995), chapter 16; the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences’ announcement of the 2007 prize.

Production possibilities curve

MKT-1.C.1MKT-1.C.2MKT-1.C.3MKT-1.C.4MKT-1.C.5

Exam skill: 4.A, draw an accurately labeled graph or visual to represent an economic model. Graphs are scored on the free-response section, so label both axes, the curve, and every point you use.

The production possibilities curve (PPC) is a model of the trade-offs that come from allocating scarce resources. It shows the combinations of two goods an economy can produce when it uses all its resources fully, with the technology it has.

On the curve
Efficient: all resources are in use with no waste. To get more of one good you must give up some of the other.
Inside the curve
Underutilized resources, so the economy could make more of at least one good without giving up the other.
Outside the curve
Unattainable with current resources and technology.
Opportunity cost
What you give up to get more of something. On a PPC it is the amount of one good given up per unit gained of the other: Δ(good given up) / Δ(good gained).
Shape
A straight line means constant opportunity cost. Bowed outward means increasing opportunity cost, because resources are not equally good at making both goods. The framework also allows decreasing opportunity cost (bowed inward).
Shifts
More or better factors of production, or better technology, shift the curve outward (economic growth). A loss of resources shifts it inward (contraction).

Three common traps

  • Moving along is not shifting. Moving along the curve shows a trade-off. A shift shows a change in what the economy is able to produce.
  • Idle resources move the point, not the curve. A recession that leaves factories and workers idle puts the economy inside the curve. The curve itself shifts inward only if productive capacity is lost.
  • Technology for one good rotates the curve. Better laptop technology raises the laptop intercept only. The phone intercept stays put.

The slope is a marginal rate of transformation

In calculus terms, the opportunity cost at a point on the frontier is the slope: the marginal rate of transformation, MRT = −dy/dx. If the frontier comes from a cost function, MRT = MCx / MCy: the ratio of marginal costs. For a quarter-circle frontier x2 + y2 = R2 the slope is −x/y, so the opportunity cost of a phone rises as you make more phones.

The frontier says what is possible. Which point society should pick depends on preferences. With utility U = xαy1−α, the best point is where the willingness to trade off the two goods, the MRS, equals the rate at which production can trade them off, the MRT. Drag along the frontier to find it.

Show the derivation
Maximize U = xαy1−α, or equivalently α ln x + (1 − α) ln y, subject to x2 + y2 = R2: L = α ln x + (1 − α) ln y + λ(R2 − x2 − y2) α/x = 2λx     (1 − α)/y = 2λy Dividing the two conditions: MRS = αy / ((1 − α)x) = x/y = MRT Substituting into the constraint gives x* = R√α and y* = R√(1 − α). Preferences choose the point; the frontier supplies the constraint.

A note on shapes. A bowed-out frontier (increasing opportunity cost) corresponds to a convex production set, where first-order conditions like the one above also identify a maximum. A bowed-in frontier means decreasing opportunity cost, which reflects increasing returns to specialization. The region under the frontier is then not a convex set, and tangency alone no longer guarantees a maximum: it is one only if the frontier bends less sharply than the indifference curve does. This is the same increasing-returns logic that appeared with non-rival ideas in topic 1.1.

Go further: the slope of the frontier as MCx/MCy and the efficiency condition MRS = MRT appear in MIT OpenCourseWare 14.01 (Chen), lecture 20, and in general-equilibrium chapters of graduate microeconomics texts.

Comparative advantage and trade

MKT-2.A.1MKT-2.A.2MKT-2.B.1MKT-2.B.2

Exam skill: 1.C, identify an economic concept using quantitative data or calculations. Expect tables of output per hour or hours per unit, and questions that ask who should produce what.

Scarcity can be eased by specializing in production and then trading. The key idea is that the best person to produce something is the one with the lowest opportunity cost, which is not always the one who is most productive.

Absolute advantage
Being able to produce more of a good with the same quantity of resources (or the same amount with fewer). It compares productivity.
Comparative advantage
Being able to produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than another producer. It compares what each gives up.
Terms of trade
The exchange rate between the two goods, such as 3 pies for 4 cakes. A trade is mutually beneficial only if the terms lie between the two producers’ opportunity costs.
Gains from trade
Specializing by comparative advantage and trading at suitable terms lets each side consume combinations beyond its own PPC.

How to calculate it

The opportunity cost of Y is the reciprocal of the opportunity cost of X. Whoever has the lower opportunity cost of X has the higher opportunity cost of Y, so each producer has a comparative advantage in one good (unless costs are identical).

Three common traps

  • Absolute advantage does not decide who produces what. A producer who is best at everything still gains by trading, because their time is more valuable in the thing they do relatively best.
  • The price has to sit strictly between the two opportunity costs. At either end, one side gains nothing.
  • In hours-per-unit tables, smaller means better. Fewer hours per unit is an absolute advantage, which flips the direction of the comparison.

From two goods to a continuum of goods

The AP trade model, with straight-line PPCs, is the two-good Ricardian model. Ricardo’s 1817 example used wine and cloth in England and Portugal. Real economies trade thousands of goods, and Dornbusch, Fischer, and Samuelson (1977) handled that by indexing goods on a continuum z from 0 to 1, ordered by home’s comparative advantage. Let A(z) be foreign labor needed per unit divided by home labor needed per unit, which falls as z rises. With home wage w and foreign wage w*, home makes a good whenever A(z) ≥ w/w*. The cutoff z̄ is where A(z̄) = w/w*.

The relative wage is pinned down by balanced trade. In this simplified version, both countries spend equally across all goods, so home exports are z̄w*L* and imports are (1 − z̄)wL. Setting them equal gives w/w* = z̄L* / ((1 − z̄)L). Try it: a bigger partner raises your wage relative to theirs but narrows the set of goods you make.

Show the derivation
Assume A(z) = eθ(0.5 − z), so A = 1 at z = 0.5 and θ controls how sharply comparative advantage varies across goods. Home produces goods z ≤ z̄. Home income is wL and foreign income is w*L*, each spent uniformly over [0, 1]. Exports = z̄ w*L*     Imports = (1 − z̄) wL Balanced trade: w/w* = z̄L* / ((1 − z̄)L)     and     w/w* = A(z̄) The left side of A(z̄) = z̄L*/((1 − z̄)L) falls in z̄ while the right side rises, so there is exactly one solution, found here numerically. A larger L* shifts the right side up, so z̄ falls and w/w* rises.

Two more connections. With increasing opportunity costs (a bowed-out PPC), countries usually specialize only partly, because the cost of the next unit keeps rising until it matches the world price. And the AP rule that the price must fall between the two opportunity costs is the two-good version of the cutoff condition above.

Go further: Ricardo, On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation (1817); Dornbusch, Fischer, and Samuelson, “Comparative Advantage, Trade, and Payments in a Ricardian Model with a Continuum of Goods,” American Economic Review 67(5), 1977, pp. 823–839. The balanced-trade condition is derived in MIT OpenCourseWare 14.581 (Donaldson), lecture 2.

Cost-benefit analysis

CBA-1.A.1CBA-1.A.2CBA-1.B.1CBA-1.B.2

Exam skill: 1.C, identify an economic concept using quantitative data or calculations. Expect to total up explicit and implicit costs and compare them with total benefits.

Rational decision makers weigh what a choice gives them against what it costs them. Because every choice means giving something up, the true cost of a decision is its opportunity cost.

Explicit cost
A cost you pay in money, such as tuition, rent, or materials.
Implicit cost
The value of what you give up when you use resources you already own, such as your own time. No money changes hands, but it is still a cost.
Total economic cost
Explicit costs plus implicit costs. This is what rational agents compare against benefits.
Total benefit
The metric is utility for consumers and total revenue for firms.
Net benefit
Total benefit minus total cost. It is largest at the optimal choice.
All-or-nothing decisions
Some decisions can be made in small steps, so marginal benefit and marginal cost are enough. Others cannot be broken into increments, such as whether to enroll in a program, and must be judged by total benefits against total costs.

Three common traps

  • Forgetting implicit costs. The wages you give up while in school are part of the cost of school, even though you never write a check for them.
  • Judging by cost alone. A choice is worth it when its net benefit is positive, and the best choice is the one with the greatest net benefit. A cheap option with small benefits can lose to a costly one with large benefits.
  • Using marginal analysis on an all-or-nothing choice. If you cannot do a little bit of it, compare totals.

Costs and benefits that arrive at different times

The AP model adds up costs and benefits as if a dollar next decade is worth a dollar today. In practice, a dollar later is worth less than a dollar now, so analysts discount. The net present value of paying C now for a benefit b each year for N years is NPV(r) = −C + Σ b/(1 + r)t, where r is the discount rate. The AP rule “total benefit greater than total cost” is the special case r = 0. The internal rate of return is the r at which NPV = 0.

Show the derivation
The benefits form an annuity. Summing the geometric series: Σt=1N b/(1 + r)t = b · (1 − (1 + r)−N) / r so NPV(r) = −C + b(1 − (1 + r)−N)/r     and     NPV(0) = Nb − C The slope dNPV/dr is negative whenever benefits are positive, so NPV falls as r rises and crosses zero at most once, at the IRR. In continuous time the same idea is PV = ∫0N b e−rt dt = b(1 − e−rN)/r.

Whose costs and whose benefits? A project can pass a total-benefits-exceed-total-costs test while some people lose. The Kaldor–Hicks criterion (both from 1939) calls a change an improvement if the winners could compensate the losers and still be better off, even if they do not. A change that actually compensates everyone is a Pareto improvement. Critics ask why a hypothetical payment should matter if it is never made. That tension is why cost-benefit analysis is often paired with a separate look at who gains and who pays.

Go further: Kaldor, “Welfare Propositions of Economics and Interpersonal Comparisons of Utility” and Hicks, “The Foundations of Welfare Economics,” both Economic Journal (1939). Discounting and NPV appear in any corporate-finance text; the uniqueness of the IRR for a cost followed by only benefits follows from Descartes’ rule of signs, and Norstrøm, “A Sufficient Condition for a Unique Nonnegative Internal Rate of Return,” Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis 7, 1972, gives a simple sufficient condition based on cumulative cash flows.

Marginal analysis and consumer choice

CBA-2.A.1CBA-2.A.2CBA-2.A.3CBA-2.A.4CBA-2.B.1CBA-2.B.2CBA-2.B.3

Exam skill: 2.C, interpret a specific economic outcome using quantitative data or calculations. The course framework says applying consumer theory with numbers is a frequent challenge on the exam, so practice showing your work. Indifference curves are outside the course, but equating the ratios of marginal utility to price is in scope.

Most decisions are not all-or-nothing. They are about how much. Marginal analysis compares the additional benefit of doing a little more with the additional cost. If marginal benefit (MB) is greater than marginal cost (MC), do more. If it is less, do less. At equality, stay put.

Total utility (TU)
The total satisfaction from all the units consumed.
Marginal utility (MU)
The extra utility from one more unit.
Diminishing marginal utility
Each additional unit adds less utility than the one before. It is one of the standard explanations, along with the income and substitution effects, for why demand curves slope downward, and it is a reason you stop buying at some point.
Consumer choice
Consumers face an income constraint and prices, and are assumed to choose the bundle that maximizes total utility.
Utility-maximizing rule
Spend all income so that the marginal utility per dollar is equal across goods: MUx/Px = MUy/Py. The last dollar should do equal work everywhere. This is the condition for divisible goods and an interior choice; with whole units, compare the affordable bundles, and at a corner the ratios need not be equal.
Sunk cost
A cost already paid that cannot be recovered. The optimal quantity does not depend on fixed costs or fixed benefits that past choices have already settled.

Four common traps

  • MU is not TU. Total utility keeps rising while marginal utility falls. It stops rising when MU reaches zero.
  • Compare MU per dollar, not raw MU. A good with a bigger MU can still be the worse buy if it costs more.
  • Sunk costs do not count. The money is gone whatever you do next, so it should not change the decision.
  • The rule needs two things. In the standard model with divisible goods, ratios must be equal and all income must be spent. With whole units, compare affordable bundles: the best one may not make the ratios exactly equal.

The multiplier is the marginal utility of income

The AP rule MUx/Px = MUy/Py comes straight out of the Lagrangian for the consumer’s problem, and the common value is the multiplier λ: the marginal utility of one more dollar of income. This is the same shadow price from topic 1.1.

The AP model treats utility as a number you can add up. Modern theory treats it as ordinal: only the ranking of bundles matters (Hicks and Allen, 1934). Test that below. Switch the utility function to a rescaled or transformed version that ranks bundles identically. Every marginal utility changes, but the best bundle does not, because the rule only needs the ratio MUx/MUy to equal the price ratio.

Show the derivation
Maximize u(x, y) subject to pxx + pyy = B: L = u(x, y) + λ(B − pxx − pyy) ux = λpx     uy = λpy     so     ux/px = uy/py = λ With u = α ln x + (1 − α) ln y this gives x* = αB/px and y* = (1 − α)B/py. Now take any increasing transformation v = g(u). Then vx = g′(u)ux and vy = g′(u)uy. The common factor g′(u) cancels in vx/vy = ux/uy = px/py, so the optimum is unchanged, while the multiplier becomes g′(u)λ.

A subtlety. Diminishing marginal utility itself is not preserved by every transformation. With u = √x marginal utility falls, but u² = x ranks bundles identically and has constant marginal utility. What survives is the marginal rate of substitution. Diminishing MRS (convex preferences) is a separate assumption about preferences; diminishing marginal utility alone does not guarantee it. That is why modern textbooks use the MRS instead.

Go further: Hicks and Allen, “A Reconsideration of the Theory of Value,” Economica (1934, in two parts). The multiplier as the marginal utility of income is in MIT OpenCourseWare 14.03 (Autor), lecture 4; that its numerical value depends on the utility scale is noted there as well.

The sunk-cost effect: when people ignore the rule

Marginal analysis says to ignore sunk costs. Real people often do not. In a field study of theater season tickets, Arkes and Blumer (1985) found that customers who had paid more for their season subscription attended more plays over the next six months, which they linked to a desire not to appear wasteful. The AP model describes what a rational agent would do. Behavioral economics studies how and why actual choices differ, and often uses the AP benchmark to measure the gap.

Go further: Arkes and Blumer, “The Psychology of Sunk Cost,” Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes 35, 1985, pp. 124–140.

Demand

MKT-3.A.1MKT-3.A.2MKT-3.A.3MKT-3.A.4MKT-3.A.5MKT-3.A.6MKT-3.B.1

Exam skill: 4.A, draw an accurately labeled graph or visual to represent an economic model or market. Label the axes, label each curve, and show the direction of any shift.

Demand describes how buyers respond to prices. Buyers respond to incentives, such as prices, but they also face constraints, such as income, time, and legal and regulatory rules. Markets work well only when property rights are well defined, because buyers and sellers need to know what they are trading.

Quantity demanded
The amount buyers are willing and able to buy at a particular price. “Able” is where the income constraint enters.
Law of demand

Where a demand curve comes from: income and substitution effects

A demand curve is the solution to the consumer’s problem from topic 1.6, solved at many prices. With utility u = xαy1−α, income m, and prices p and py, demand for x is x(p, m) = αm/p. It slopes down, and it shifts right when m rises, which is exactly the AP determinant “income, normal good.”

A price change moves demand in two ways. The substitution effect is the change if the consumer is compensated so that utility stays the same. It always points the “right” way: a higher price lowers quantity. The income effect is the rest, caused by the price change shrinking or stretching purchasing power. For a normal good it also points the right way. For an inferior good it pushes the other way, and if it is strong enough, demand slopes up. That rare case is a Giffen good.

Show the derivation
The Slutsky equation splits the slope of Marshallian demand x(p, m) using Hicksian (compensated) demand h(p, u): ∂x/∂p = ∂h/∂p − x · ∂x/∂m The first term is the substitution effect (negative). The second is the income effect: minus the quantity bought times how demand responds to income. For Cobb–Douglas, x = αm/p, so ∂x/∂p = −αm/p2     ∂h/∂p = −(1 − α)αm/p2     x · ∂x/∂m = α2m/p2 and indeed −(1 − α)αm/p2 − α2m/p2 = −αm/p2. So for a small price change, a share 1 − α of the response is substitution and a share α is income. The more of your budget you spend on a good, the bigger its income effect.

Giffen behavior is rare. Jensen and Miller (2008) found the first real-world evidence, from a field experiment that subsidized rice prices for very poor households in Hunan province, China. They stress it is the consumers’ behavior, not the good itself, and the overall market did not show it.

Why supply is the marginal cost curve

A firm that takes the price as given chooses output to maximize profit π = pq − C(q). The first-order condition is p = MC(q): produce until the marginal cost of the last unit equals the price. So the firm’s supply curve is its marginal cost curve (above the shutdown price), which is why the AP graphs label the supply curve “Supply (MC).” Market supply adds the firms sideways.

Elasticity as a log-derivative, and the revenue-maximizing price

For a small change, elasticity is a derivative: ε = (dQ/dP)(P/Q) = d ln Q / d ln P. That log form explains why economists estimate “log-log” models: the coefficient on ln P is the elasticity. A demand curve with the same elasticity everywhere has the form Q = AP−ε, a curve that looks nothing like a straight line.

Total revenue R = PQ changes with price at the rate dR/dP = Q(1 + ε), with ε negative. So revenue rises with price when demand is inelastic, is flat at ε = −1, and falls when demand is elastic. Marginal revenue is MR = P(1 + 1/ε), which is positive only on the elastic part of demand. Topic 4.2 will use that.

Show the derivation
Revenue R(P) = P · Q(P). By the product rule, dR/dP = Q + P · dQ/dP = Q(1 + (dQ/dP)(P/Q)) = Q(1 + ε) Revenue is maximized where dR/dP = 0, so ε = −1. For linear demand Q = a − bP, ε = −bP/(a − bP), which equals −1 at P = a/(2b), the midpoint of the curve. For constant elasticity, R = AP1−ε′ (writing ε′ = −ε > 0), so revenue rises in price if ε′ < 1, is constant if ε′ = 1, and falls if ε′ > 1. With respect to quantity, MR = dR/dQ = P + Q · dP/dQ = P(1 + 1/ε).

Elasticities decide who bears a tax

Who pays when a per-unit tax is added to a good? Not necessarily the side the law names. The burden falls more on whichever side is less responsive, because that side has fewer ways to avoid the tax. With a small tax, the consumers’ share of the burden is εS / (εS + |εD|). This is previewed in the framework’s later topic on taxes (topic 2.8), where incidence depends on the elasticities of supply and demand.

Show the derivation
Let demand and supply be locally linear around the pre-tax point (P0, Q0) with elasticities εD < 0 and εS > 0: QD = Q0 + εD(Q0/P0)(Pc − P0)     QS = Q0 + εS(Q0/P0)(Ps − P0) With a tax t, buyers pay Pc and sellers keep Ps = Pc − t. Estimating elasticities from data: the identification problem

In practice, elasticities are estimated. A log-log regression ln Q = β0 + βP ln P + βY ln Y + βX ln PX + u gives each elasticity directly as a coefficient: βP is the price elasticity, βY the income elasticity, and βX the cross-price elasticity.

But there is a trap. In real markets, price and quantity are set together by supply and demand, so each observed point is an intersection of two curves. Regressing quantity on price then traces something between the demand and supply curves, not demand. Elmer Working made this point in 1927. The fix is a variable that shifts only one curve, called an instrument. Try it below.

Show the derivation
In logs, with constants dropped, let demand be q = −eDp + uD and supply be q = eSp − z + uS, where z is a cost shifter that moves supply but not demand. Market clearing gives p = (uD − uS + z) / (eS + eD) Price is correlated with the demand shock uD, so ordinary least squares is biased. With independent shocks of variance σD2, σS2 and Var(z) = 1, plim βOLS = −eD + σD2(eS + eD) / (σD2 + σS2 + 1) The estimate is pulled toward the supply slope when demand shocks dominate. The instrumental-variables estimator uses only the part of price movement caused by z: βIV = Cov(z, q) / Cov(z, p) = −eD because z is uncorrelated with uD. If prices are instead set independently of demand shocks (for example in a randomized price experiment), OLS recovers the demand elasticity directly.

Go further: Working, “What Do Statistical ‘Demand Curves’ Show?” Quarterly Journal of Economics 41(2), 1927, pp. 212–235; Angrist and Krueger, “Instrumental Variables and the Search for Identification: From Supply and Demand to Natural Experiments” (2001, NBER Working Paper 8456).

Market equilibrium and consumer and producer surplus

MKT-4.A.1MKT-4.A.2MKT-4.A.3MKT-4.A.4MKT-4.A.5

Exam skill: 2.A, explain how a specific economic outcome occurs using economic concepts, principles, or models. The framework flags surplus calculations as a frequent challenge: students know the triangle formula but find the area of the wrong triangle.

Put supply and demand together and you get a market price. In a perfectly competitive market, the price settles where buyers want to buy exactly what sellers want to sell. That price also carries information: it tells buyers and sellers what the good is worth at the margin and guides how resources are allocated.

Market equilibrium
The price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. The market clears: there is no shortage and no surplus.
Consumer surplus
What buyers are willing to pay minus what they actually pay, summed over the units bought. On a graph it is the area below the demand curve and above the price.
Producer surplus
What sellers receive minus the least they would accept (their cost), summed over the units sold. On a graph it is the area above the supply curve and below the price.
Total economic surplus
Consumer surplus plus producer surplus. Without market failures, the competitive equilibrium maximizes it, which is why perfectly competitive markets are called efficient.

How big is the effect? And does the market ever find equilibrium?

Around an equilibrium, with elasticities εD < 0 and εS > 0, a horizontal shift of demand by δ percent and of supply by σ percent gives %ΔP = (δ − σ)/(εS + |εD|) and %ΔQ = σ + εS · %ΔP. This is exact for linear curves. It shows why the framework says the impact depends on the elasticities, and why with both curves shifting the sign of one change is ambiguous.

The AP model assumes the market jumps to equilibrium. If producers must commit to output before they see the price, adjustment can overshoot. This is the cobweb model: supply responds to last period’s price, demand to this period’s. Prices spiral in if demand is more elastic than supply at the equilibrium, cycle if the elasticities are equal, and spiral out if supply is more elastic.

Show the derivation
Elasticity form of the comparative statics. Let demand be Q = Q0(1 + δ) + εD(Q0/P0)(P − P0) and supply Q = Q0(1 + σ) + εS(Q0/P0)(P − P0). Setting them equal: (P − P0)/P0 = (δ − σ)/(εS − εD) Cobweb: with QSt = Q0 + εS(Q0/P0)(Pt−1 − P0) and demand clearing the market each period, Pt − P0 = −(εS/|εD|)(Pt−1 − P0) This is a first-order difference equation with factor r = −εS/|εD|. Deviations shrink if |r| < 1, repeat if |r| = 1, and grow if |r| > 1, always alternating in sign.

Governments change market outcomes with price controls, quantity limits, taxes, and subsidies. The framework’s central claim: in a market that already produces the efficient quantity, any of these interventions can only decrease allocative efficiency. The loss shows up as deadweight loss.

Price ceiling
A legal maximum price. It matters only if it is set below equilibrium. Then quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied: a shortage.
Price floor
A legal minimum price. It matters only if it is set above equilibrium. Then quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded: a surplus.
Quantity regulation
A legal limit on the quantity traded. If it is below the equilibrium quantity, buyers end up paying the price they are willing to pay for that smaller quantity.
Per-unit tax
Drives a wedge between the price buyers pay and the price sellers keep. Quantity falls. Government revenue is the tax times the new quantity.
Per-unit subsidy
Drives the wedge the other way: buyers pay less, sellers keep more, and quantity rises above the efficient level. Government cost is the subsidy times the new quantity.
Tax incidence
Who actually bears the tax. In a competitive market it depends on the elasticities of supply and demand, not on who the law requires to pay.
Deadweight loss
The surplus lost on units that would have been worth producing but are no longer traded.

  • A control that is not binding does nothing. A ceiling above equilibrium or a floor below it changes nothing.

Why deadweight loss grows with the square of the tax

For a small tax t, the deadweight loss is the Harberger triangle: height t, base ΔQ, so DWL = ½ t ΔQ. Because ΔQ itself grows in proportion to t, the deadweight loss grows roughly with t2: double the tax and the loss roughly quadruples. Revenue, t(Q0 − ht), rises at first but then falls as the shrinking quantity outweighs the higher rate. This is one standard argument for broad tax bases with moderate rates, though fairness and administration matter too. When several goods must be taxed to raise a given revenue and the only goal is to minimize efficiency cost, Ramsey’s rule says to reduce the compensated demand for every good by the same proportion. If demands are independent (zero cross-elasticities), that becomes the inverse-elasticity rule: tax goods with less elastic demand at higher rates. Equity concerns can change the prescription, since necessities tend to have inelastic demand.

Show the derivation
With linear curves, demand Q = a − d Pb and supply Q = k Ps, a tax t separates the prices by Pb − Ps = t. Solving gives Q(t) = Q0 − h t with h = dk/(d + k), the combined slope. DWL(t) = ½ t · ΔQ = ½ h t2     R(t) = t(Q0 − ht) Revenue is largest at t = Q0/(2h), half the tax that would shut the market down (t = Q0/h). Past that point, a higher rate raises less revenue and still costs more deadweight loss. Doubling a small t multiplies DWL by four. The exact quadratic holds for linear curves; for general curves it is an approximation for small taxes.

Cautions. This is a one-market, partial-equilibrium picture. Accurate welfare measurement in real data uses compensated demand curves and accounts for other markets, and the triangle can be a poor approximation when taxes interact with other distortions.

When a big country taxes imports: the terms-of-trade argument

The AP model assumes the importing country is too small to move the world price. Drop that assumption: if foreign exporters’ supply slopes upward, a tariff cuts the importer’s demand and pushes the world price down, so foreign exporters bear part of the tariff. That terms-of-trade gain can outweigh the two deadweight-loss triangles at a small tariff, so the importing country can come out ahead, even though total world surplus falls. The less elastic the foreign export supply, the larger the optimal tariff, and for an infinitely elastic export supply (the small-country case) the optimal tariff is zero.

Show the derivation
Home import demand is M(P) = Qd(P) − Qs(P) = 120 − 24P. Foreign export supply is linear through the free-trade point (world price $3, imports 48), with elasticity εX at that point: X(Pw) = 48 + 16εX(Pw − 3). With a specific tariff t, the home price is Pd = Pw + t, and imports equal exports: 120 − 24(Pw + t) = 48 + 16εX(Pw − 3)     so     Pw = (72 + 48εX − 24t)/(24 + 16εX) The world price falls by 24t/(24 + 16εX), a smaller drop as εX grows. Home’s welfare change is ΔCS + ΔPS + tariff revenue. Foreign exporters lose 48(3 − Pw) − 8εX(3 − Pw)2. The sum of the two is negative for every tariff, which is the sense in which tariffs reduce world surplus.

Cautions. This is the textbook logic, and it needs strong conditions: market power over world prices and no retaliation. Johnson (1953–54) showed a country can gain from a tariff even after retaliation if its market power is large enough. The evidence is mixed. Broda, Limão and Weinstein (2008) found that countries outside the WTO set systematically higher tariffs on goods that foreign suppliers provide inelastically, consistent with exploiting market power. But studies of the 2018 US tariffs (Amiti, Redding and Weinstein, 2019) found near-complete pass-through into US import prices, which means almost no terms-of-trade gain, and a 2025 Richmond Fed brief explains why real-world conditions usually undercut the case.

3.1

The production function

PRD-1.A.1PRD-1.A.2PRD-1.A.3

Exam skill: 1.A, describe economic concepts, principles, or models. Know the vocabulary, read a production table, and recognize where diminishing marginal returns begin.

A firm turns inputs, such as labor and capital, into output. How much output it gets from a given set of inputs is described by its production function. This topic looks at what happens to output as the firm uses more of one input while the others stay fixed.

Production function
The relationship between inputs and output, in both the short run and the long run.
Short run
A period in which at least one input (often capital, like a factory or equipment) is fixed. The firm can change output only by changing its variable inputs, like labor. It is defined by what can be adjusted, not by the calendar.
Long run
A period long enough for the firm to adjust all of its inputs.
Total product (TP)
The total output produced with a given amount of the variable input.
Marginal product (MP)
The extra output from one more unit of the variable input: MP = ΔTP ÷ ΔL. It is the slope of the total product curve.
Average product (AP)
Output per unit of the variable input: AP = TP ÷ L.
Diminishing marginal returns
As a firm employs more of one input, holding the others constant, the marginal product of that input eventually falls.
If…Then…
MP > 0Total product is rising
MP = 0Total product is at its maximum
MP < 0Total product is falling (negative marginal returns)
MP > APAverage product is rising
MP < APAverage product is falling

The usual pattern. Many textbooks, including OpenStax, note that marginal product may rise at first, for example because workers specialize, and then fall. The framework requires you to know the part where it falls: diminishing marginal returns.

Common traps

  • Diminishing returns does not mean output is falling. Marginal product can be falling while still positive, so total product keeps rising, just more slowly.
  • Marginal and average are different. MP is the add-on from the last worker. AP is the overall average. MP pulls AP up when it is above and drags it down when it is below.
  • Short run versus long run is about fixed inputs. It is not a number of months or years.
  • Marginal returns are not returns to scale. Diminishing marginal returns come from adding one input to fixed others (short run). Returns to scale ask what happens when all inputs increase (long run, topic 3.3).

Beyond the courseUses derivatives

Marginal product as a derivative, and why MP crosses AP at its peak

With output Q = f(L), marginal product is the derivative MP = dQ/dL and average product is AP = Q/L. A useful identity ties them together: d(AP)/dL = (MP − AP)/L. So average product rises when marginal product is above it, falls when below it, and peaks exactly where MP = AP. The same logic will return in topic 3.2 for average and marginal cost.

The shape of the production function matters. A cubic Q = aL2 − bL3 has increasing marginal returns at first, then diminishing, then negative. A Cobb–Douglas form Q = ALβ with β < 1 has diminishing marginal returns from the very first worker, and its MP/AP = β at every point, so MP never crosses AP.

Show the derivation
d(AP)/dL = d(Q/L)/dL = (L · Q′ − Q)/L2 = (MP − AP)/L For the cubic, MP = 2aL − 3bL2 and AP = aL − bL2. Three landmarks, in order: MP is largest at L = a/(3b)     AP is largest at L = a/(2b)     TP is largest at L = 2a/(3b) At L = a/(2b), MP = AP: setting 2aL − 3bL2 = aL − bL2 gives L = a/(2b). For Cobb–Douglas, MP = βALβ−1 = β · AP, so MP is always the fraction β of AP and falls from the start.

3.2

Short-run production costs

PRD-1.A.4PRD-1.A.5PRD-1.A.6PRD-1.A.7PRD-1.A.8

Exam skill: 4.A, draw an accurately labeled graph or visual to represent an economic model or market. Expect to draw MC, ATC, AVC, and AFC with the right relationships between them.

Production decisions come with costs. In the short run some inputs are fixed, so some costs are too. The shapes of the cost curves follow from the production function in topic 3.1.

Total fixed cost (TFC)
Costs that do not change with output, such as rent. They stay the same at every output level, including zero output.
Total variable cost (TVC)
Costs that change with output, such as materials and hourly labor. Zero at zero output.
Total cost (TC)
TC = TFC + TVC.

Why the curves have the shapes they do

  • MC rises because of diminishing marginal returns. When each extra worker adds less output, each extra unit takes more labor, so it costs more. When labor is the only variable input, MC = wage ÷ MP: MC is the mirror image of MP.
  • MC may fall at first. Specialization and the division of labor can lower marginal cost at low output, before diminishing returns take over.
  • AFC always falls. The same fixed cost is spread over more units.
  • ATC and AVC are typically U-shaped. They fall while MC is below them and rise when MC is above them, so MC crosses each at its minimum point. ATC is above AVC by exactly AFC, and the gap narrows as output rises.
  • Cost curves shift. A higher price for a variable input (such as the wage) shifts MC, AVC, and ATC up. Better productivity shifts them down. A higher fixed cost shifts AFC and ATC, but not MC or AVC.

Common traps

  • Fixed cost is not zero at zero output. The firm still pays its rent.
  • MC crosses ATC and AVC at their minimums, not at MC’s own minimum. The minimum of ATC is at a higher output than the minimum of AVC.
  • Total cost and per-unit cost belong on different graphs. MC, AVC, ATC, and AFC are per-unit curves.

Cost is the mirror image of production

If labor is the only variable input, and its price is the wage w, then total variable cost is wL. Divide by output Q and you get AVC = w/AP. The change in cost for one more unit gives MC = w/MP. So the cost curves are the production curves turned upside down: the output where AP peaks is the output where AVC is lowest, and wherever MP is high, MC is low. Fixed cost F adds F/Q to the average, so ATC = (F + wL)/Q bottoms out at a larger output than AVC.

Show the derivation
Let Q = f(L) and TVC = wL. AVC = wL/Q = w/AP     MC = dTVC/dQ = w · (dL/dQ) = w/MP Since d(AVC)/dQ = (MC − AVC)/Q, AVC is at its minimum exactly where MC = AVC. With AVC = w/AP, that is where AP is at its maximum, which is where MP = AP. For the cubic Q = aL2 − bL3 that is L = a/(2b), and the minimum AVC is w/(a2/(4b)) = 4bw/a2. Long-run production costs

In the short run some inputs are stuck. In the long run, the firm can change everything: build a bigger factory, buy more machines, hire more people. That changes what costs look like.

Long run
All inputs can be adjusted, so all costs are variable. There is no fixed cost.
Returns to scale
What happens to output when all inputs are increased by the same proportion. Increasing returns: output rises by a larger proportion. Constant: the same proportion. Decreasing: a smaller proportion.
Long-run average total cost (LRATC)
The lowest average cost of producing each output when the firm can choose its plant size. It is the lower edge, or “envelope,” of the short-run ATC curves for all possible plant sizes.
Economies of scale
LRATC falls as output rises.
Constant returns to scale (efficient scale)
LRATC is flat. The framework pairs these two labels. Strictly, constant returns to scale describes output rising in the same proportion as all inputs (with constant input prices, that gives a flat LRATC), while efficient scale is the output range at the minimum of LRATC. A flat bottom is a range, and its smallest output is the minimum efficient scale below.
Diseconomies of scale
LRATC rises as output rises.
Minimum efficient scale (MES)
The smallest output at which LRATC reaches its lowest level. It helps determine how many firms a market can support, and so its market structure.

Why scale effects happen. Commonly cited reasons for economies of scale are specialization of workers and machines, spreading costs that do not grow in proportion to size, and bulk purchasing. Diseconomies often come from the difficulty of managing and coordinating a very large organization. These are typical explanations, not laws.

Common traps

  • Diminishing marginal returns is not diseconomies of scale. Diminishing returns: add one input, hold the others fixed (short run). Diseconomies of scale: change all inputs (long run). A firm can have both at once: a rising short-run MC curve and a falling LRATC.

  • LRATC is not the short-run ATC. Each short-run ATC belongs to one plant size and touches LRATC at one point, lying above it everywhere else.
  • MES is a quantity. It is the output where LRATC stops falling, not a price.

Beyond the courseUses derivatives and constrained minimization

Scale elasticity: average cost divided by marginal cost

Returns to scale have a precise cost-side measure. If C(Q) is the long-run cost of producing Q, then average cost is AC = C/Q and marginal cost is MC = C′(Q). The ratio S = AC/MC is the scale elasticity: above 1, average cost is falling (economies of scale); equal to 1, flat; below 1, rising. For a Cobb–Douglas technology Q = AKαLβ, the firm minimizes wL + rK for each Q, and the result is simple: S = α + β, the degree of homogeneity of the production function. So returns to scale and economies of scale coincide here, and input prices only change the level of cost, not its shape.

Show the derivation
Minimizing wL + rK subject to AKαLβ = Q gives the tangency condition K/L = (α/β)(w/r). Substituting, L* = [ (Q/A)(βr/(αw))α ]1/(α+β)     K* = (αw/(βr)) L*     C(Q) = wL* + rK* = BQ1/(α+β) for a constant B that depends on A, α, β, w, r. Then MC = C′ = C/((α+β)Q), so AC/MC = α + β. If α + β > 1, the exponent 1/(α+β) is below 1, so cost is concave in output and AC falls.

The “envelope” in the framework’s LRATC is the envelope theorem at work. If SRATC(q; s) is average cost with plant size s and LRATC(q) = mins SRATC(q; s), then at the best plant size d(LRATC)/dq = ∂SRATC/∂q. So each short-run curve is tangent to LRATC at the output it is chosen for, and short-run marginal cost equals long-run marginal cost there. Where LRATC is falling, the tangency is to the left of that plant’s own minimum-ATC point. This is the standard smooth case. With corner solutions or special production functions the tangency need not hold (Dassiou and Glycopantis, Economics Letters, 2017).

Out-of-pocket payments: wages, rent paid, materials, interest on a loan.

Economic profit in corporate finance: the capital charge

Economic profit has a close cousin in finance. Economic value added (EVA, a trademark of Stern Stewart) is NOPAT − WACC × invested capital: operating profit after tax minus a charge for all the capital used, at its cost. It is a residual-income-style measure, and the idea is the same: a firm creates value only if it earns more than its capital could have earned elsewhere at the same risk. Stern Stewart’s EVA applies its own conventions and accounting adjustments, so it can differ from simple residual income. Net income alone cannot tell you this, because it ignores the cost of equity.

For a single owner, the same logic applies in simpler form. The capital charge is the required return times the owner’s capital. One common model for the required return on equity is the capital asset pricing model: r = rf + β(rm − rf). A riskier venture (higher β) has a higher implicit cost of capital, which is exactly the framework’s “compensation for risk” in implicit costs.

Show the derivation
Start from the AP definitions: economic profit = accounting profit − implicit costs. For an owner who supplies time and equity capital E, implicit costs are the forgone wage w0 plus the capital charge rE: πecon = πacct − w0 − rE     with     r = rf + β(rm − rf) The break-even risk level, where πecon = 0, is β* = [(πacct − w0)/E − rf] / (rm − rf). For a firm with debt, EVA works with operating profit before financing costs (NOPAT) and charges the weighted average cost of capital on total capital, so debt and equity are each charged once.

Caveats: CAPM is one model of required returns, not the only one, and real EVA calculations adjust accounting figures (for example, treatment of leases and research spending). OpenStax’s managerial accounting text writes EVA with after-tax income rather than NOPAT, and notes that residual income and EVA can lead to different decisions because they use different required returns and tax bases.

Go further: EVA and residual income are covered in corporate finance and managerial accounting texts; Stern Stewart introduced EVA as a refinement of the older residual-income idea.

Firms are assumed to choose output to maximize profit by comparing marginal revenue and marginal cost. This is the cost-benefit logic from Unit 1 applied to a firm.

Marginal revenue (MR)
The extra revenue from selling one more unit: MR = ΔTR ÷ ΔQ. For a price taker, every unit sells at the market price, so MR = P and the firm’s demand curve is horizontal.
Profit-maximizing rule
Produce the quantity where MR = MC (for a price taker, P = MC), with marginal cost rising at that point.
If MR > MC
One more unit adds more to revenue than to cost, so produce more.
If MR < MC
The last unit cost more than it brought in, so produce less.

Two ways to see it. In total terms, profit is the gap TR − TC, and it is largest where the slopes of the two curves match, which is where MR = MC. In per-unit terms, profit is (P − ATC) × Q, the rectangle between the price line and the ATC curve.

Common traps

  • MR = MC tells you the quantity, not whether there is a profit. Whether profit is positive depends on price versus ATC (topic 3.6).
  • It is not where P = ATC, or where ATC is lowest. A firm that cares about total profit goes past the minimum of ATC if price is above it.
  • Marginal cost must be rising at the crossing. A firm with a U-shaped MC curve can have P = MC at two quantities. The profit-maximizing one is where MC crosses price from below.
  • Use the right profit rectangle. Height is P − ATC (not P − MC) and width is the profit-maximizing quantity.

Beyond the courseUses derivatives and the envelope theorem

The profit function and Hotelling’s lemma

A price-taking firm solves maxq π(q) = Pq − C(q). The first-order condition is P = C′(q), which is MR = MC. The second-order condition is C″(q) ≥ 0: marginal cost must be rising. If q*(P) is the solution, the maximum profit is a function of price, π*(P) = Pq*(P) − C(q*(P)). Hotelling’s lemma says that, where π* is differentiable (the optimal quantity is unique), its slope is the firm’s supply: dπ*/dP = q*(P). Because supply slopes upward, π* is convex in price: when the price rises, profit rises by at least what the firm would gain without changing its output, and by more when it adjusts.

Show the derivation
With C(q) = F + cq + (a/2)q2: MC = c + aq, so q*(P) = (P − c)/a for P ≥ c, and π*(P) = (P − c)2/(2a) − F Differentiating, dπ*/dP = (P − c)/a = q*(P). In general the envelope theorem gives the same result: by the first-order condition, the indirect effect of changing q is zero, so dπ*/dP = ∂π/∂P = q*. Note (P − c)2/(2a) is exactly the producer surplus from topic 2.2, so profit equals producer surplus minus fixed cost.

Go further: Hotelling’s lemma and convexity of the profit function appear in Mas-Colell, Whinston and Green, Microeconomic Theory (chapter 5), and in Varian, Microeconomic Analysis.

Firms’ short-run decisions to produce and long-run decisions to enter or exit a market

PRD-2.A.1PRD-2.A.2

Exam skill: 2.A, explain how a specific economic outcome occurs or what action should be taken. Decide whether a firm should produce, shut down, enter, or exit.

Once a firm has found its best quantity, it asks a second question: is it worth producing at all? The answer depends on the time frame.

Short-run decision: operate or shut down
Compare total revenue with total variable cost, or equivalently price with average variable cost. If TR ≥ TVC (P ≥ AVC), operate. If not, shut down and produce zero.
Why only variable cost?
In the short run, the model assumes fixed costs already committed for the period must be paid whether or not the firm produces. Shutting down avoids only the variable cost, so the firm operates if revenue covers at least that.
Shutdown point
The minimum of the AVC curve, where MC crosses AVC. A price below it means revenue does not even cover variable cost. At exactly that price the firm is indifferent: operating and shutting down both lose exactly the fixed cost.
Firm’s short-run supply curve
The part of the MC curve above the minimum of AVC. Below that price, quantity supplied is zero.
Long-run decision: enter or exit
With no barriers, firms enter a market where there are economic profits and exit when they expect economic losses. In the long run all costs are variable, so the firm compares price with ATC.

Common traps

  • Shut down against AVC, not ATC. A firm with price between AVC and ATC loses money but should keep operating in the short run.
  • Shutting down is not free. The firm still owes its fixed costs, so its loss is TFC.
  • Shutting down and exiting are different. Shutting down (producing zero) is a short-run decision. Exit (leaving the industry) is long run.
  • Fixed costs already determined by past choices do not change the choice. The framework calls these sunk costs. The model assumes they must be paid whether or not the firm produces, so only revenue and variable cost matter. (Strictly, fixed and sunk are different ideas: a fixed cost does not vary with output, and a sunk cost cannot be recovered. A fixed cost that could be avoided by shutting down would belong in the comparison, but the AP model assumes none can be.)
  • Check the supply curve. The firm’s supply curve is MC above minimum AVC, not the whole MC curve.

Beyond the courseUses the profit function and a kink

The profit function has a kink at the shutdown price

Hotelling’s lemma (topic 3.5) says the slope of the firm’s maximized profit with respect to price is its supply. With the shutdown option, the firm’s profit is π*(P) = max{ −F, maxq [Pq − C(q)] }. Below the shutdown price the best choice is to produce nothing and lose F, so profit is flat and the slope (supply) is zero. At the shutdown price the interior solution just matches −F, and above it profit rises with slope equal to q*(P). The profit function therefore has a kink there, and the supply curve jumps from zero to the output at minimum AVC. At the kink itself the two-sided derivative does not exist: the left slope is 0 and the right slope is the minimum-AVC output (6 in this example), and at that exact price the firm is indifferent between shutting down and producing it.

Sunk costs and real options. The AP model assumes free entry and exit. When entry requires sunk cost and the price is uncertain, waiting has option value. In Dixit’s (1989) model, the price that triggers entry exceeds variable cost plus the interest on the sunk entry cost, and the price that triggers exit is below variable cost. The gap between the two is a “hysteresis” band, and it is significant even for small sunk costs.

Show the derivation
Take C(q) = F + (2/3)q3 − 8q2 + 50q, so MC = 2q2 − 16q + 50 and AVC = (2/3)q2 − 8q + 50. AVC is minimized at q = 6 with AVC = MC = 26. Setting P = MC on the rising branch gives q*(P) = 4 + √(8P − 144)/4 for P ≥ 18. At P = 26, q* = 6 and the interior profit is 26 · 6 − F − TVC(6) = 156 − 156 − F = −F. So the interior solution equals the shutdown payoff exactly at the minimum of AVC. Below it, Pq* − TVC(q*) < 0, so shutting down is better.

Go further: Dixit, “Entry and Exit Decisions under Uncertainty,” Journal of Political Economy 97(3), 1989, pp. 620–638; Hotelling’s lemma appears in graduate microeconomics texts such as Mas-Colell, Whinston and Green. The kink at the shutdown price comes from combining it with the shutdown option, as derived above.

Perfect competition

PRD-3.A.1PRD-3.A.2PRD-3.A.3PRD-3.A.4PRD-3.A.5PRD-3.A.6PRD-3.A.7PRD-3.A.8PRD-3.A.9

Exam skill: 4.A, draw an accurately labeled graph or visual to represent an economic model or market. The framework says perfect competition is frequently tested and the benchmark for the other market structures. Practice drawing the market and the firm side by side.

This topic puts everything in Unit 3 together. A perfectly competitive market is the benchmark against which monopoly and the other market structures are compared in Unit 4.

Characteristics
Many buyers and sellers, identical products, no barriers to entry or exit, and no firm with market power. Standard textbooks also assume buyers and sellers have the relevant information.
Price taker
Each firm is too small to affect the price and can sell all it wants at the market price. Its demand curve is horizontal at that price, and MR = P.
Profit maximization
The firm produces where MR = MC, which means P = MC.
Allocative efficiency
P = MC: the private marginal benefit of the last unit to buyers equals the private marginal cost of producing it. This holds for an operating firm that is a price taker.
Productive efficiency
Firms produce at the minimum of ATC (efficient scale), at the lowest possible cost per unit.
Short-run equilibrium
Price can be above or below its long-run level, so firms may earn profits or losses. That motivates entry or exit, which moves price and quantity toward long-run equilibrium.
Long-run equilibrium
Zero economic profit, P = MC = minimum ATC, and all firms operating at efficient scale. The market is both allocatively and productively efficient.
Constant-, increasing-, and decreasing-cost industries
When the industry expands, firms’ costs may stay the same, rise (for example, input prices go up as many firms bid for scarce inputs), or fall. That decides whether the long-run supply curve is flat, upward-sloping, or downward-sloping.

Scope note. The efficiency conclusion is for the model’s assumptions: price reflects private benefits and private costs, and there are no externalities or other market failures. When external costs or benefits exist (Unit 6), a competitive market can produce too much or too little.

Common traps

  • Two graphs, two sets of axes. The market graph has a downward-sloping demand curve and an upward-sloping supply curve. The firm graph has a horizontal price line, MC, ATC, and AVC. The price on the market graph becomes the height of the firm’s line.
  • Zero economic profit is not zero accounting profit. In long-run equilibrium firms still earn normal profit.
  • The firm does not choose the price. It chooses quantity. Entry and exit change the market supply, and that changes the price.
  • In long-run equilibrium the price line touches the bottom of ATC. Draw the line tangent to ATC at its minimum, where MC crosses it.
  • Short-run profit can be positive or negative. When the firm operates, it chooses output where P = MC, whether it earns a profit or a loss, but it is productively efficient only in long-run equilibrium. If price is below minimum AVC the firm shuts down and produces zero, so the interior condition P = MC need not hold.

Beyond the courseUses a Cournot model (preview of topic 4.5)

Perfect competition as the limit of many firms

Is price-taking an assumption or a result? In the Cournot model, n identical firms each choose a quantity, taking the others’ output as given. With linear demand P = A − BQ and constant marginal cost c, each firm produces q = (A − c)/(B(n + 1)) and the price is P = (A + nc)/(n + 1). As n grows, P approaches c: the market converges to the competitive outcome, where price equals marginal cost and the deadweight loss disappears. In the symmetric case the markup also has a clean form: the Lerner index (P − c)/P = 1/(n|ε|), where |ε| is the absolute value of the market demand elasticity (the signed elasticity is negative). More generally, a Cournot firm’s price–cost margin is its market share divided by the elasticity. The convergence result is for identical firms and linear demand with constant marginal cost, as here; other demand and cost shapes need conditions.

Show the derivation
Firm i maximizes (A − BQ − c)qi, so A − BQ − c − Bqi = 0. By symmetry, Q = nq, giving A − c = B(n + 1)q and the results above. Then P − c = (A − c)/(n + 1), and the competitive quantity (A − c)/B exceeds the Cournot quantity by (A − c)/(B(n + 1)), so DWL = ½(P − c)(Qc − Q) = (A − c)2/(2B(n + 1)2) falling roughly with 1/n2. With the absolute elasticity |ε| = P/(BQ) one can check (P − c)/P = 1/(n|ε|). For n = 1 this is the monopoly markup, 1/|ε|.

Go further: Cournot’s duopoly model (1838) and its convergence to perfect competition are covered in industrial organization texts, and the margin-equals-share-over-elasticity result is derived in McAfee, Introduction to Economic Analysis (open textbook), chapter 17.
